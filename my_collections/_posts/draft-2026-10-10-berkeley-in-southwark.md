### Southwark rejects a third Berkeley scheme 

xx Oct 2026

<img width="931" height="183" alt="Aylesham_Centre_Borough_Triangle_Bermondsey_Place_composite_121026" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8bc70a4c-93f4-40cb-a622-1cd3096f3954" />

Southwark Council unanimously rejected developer Berkeley's proposals for the development of Bermondsey Place Phases 2 and 3, at its planning committee meeting on 6 October.  This follows Berkeley's failure to gain approval for its proposals for the Borough Triangle, at the [beginning of September](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=650&MId=8449&Ver=4) and the Aylesham Centre, [in July 2025](https://35percent.org/2025/07/16/aylesham-centre-planning-application-rejected.html).

All three applications entailed big reductions in affordable housing, either by way of a s73 variation of an existing planning permission (reducing affordable housing by 10% on the Borough Triangle), by securing a whole new planning permission (a reduction of 12% Bermondsey Place) or by amending the planning application before it had been determined (also a reduction of 12% for the Aylesham Centre).  Had all three revisions succeeded it would have reduced the number of affordable homes from 951 to 304 - a loss of 647 affordable homes. So far, Berkeley has only appealed against the Aylesham Centre decision.  This [pre-emptive appeal](https://35percent.org/2026/06/08/aylesham-centre-decision-the-backlash.html) was lost. The outcome of a further appeal to the High Court is still awaited.  As yet there is news on whether Berkeley will appeal against the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place decisions.

### The three decisions

The reasons for the three refusals are similar, but also vary each case.  The Aylesham Centre was [refused by Southwark](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/mgAi.aspx?ID=74119) because of the low level of affordable housing, failure to re-provide retail space and because the remaining benefits of the scheme did not outweigh the harm caused to local heritage by the size of the development.  The Appeal Inspector, by contrast, was satisfied with the retail provision and 12% affordable housing, after taking into consideration the viability of the scheme. But he agreed with Southwark about the heritage harm, describing various parts of the design as *'monolithic and unrelenting'*, *'overly domineering'*, *'harmful'* visually and having an *'unduly forceful presence'*.  The Inspector emphasises how heavily poor design weighed in the balance of his decision-making by concluding that he would still have rejected the application, even if the development had provided 35% affordable housing[^1].  

The reasons for refusing the Borough Triangle appliction have not been published yet, but the officer report recommended refusing the application because the 10% affordable housing offer failed to provide enough public benefit to outweigh the heritage harm to Trinity Church Square Conservation Area, owing to *'the height, scale and massing'* of the two tallest blocks (one 38 storeys, the other 44 storeys).   

Unlike the Aylesham and Borough Triangle, the application for Bermondsey Place Phase 2 and 3 came with a recommendation to approve.  Also, the construction of 285 homes on Phase 1 is nearly finished. This will have 81 affordable homes (40% affordable).  All three phases will deliver 1,453 homes, with a total of 167 affordable homes.  This would be 18% affordable housing, made up of 40% on Phase 1, 12% on Phases 2 & 3[^2], 

The reasons for refusal, known so far, vary somewhat for each of the schemes; the Inspector, as decision-maker for the Aylesham, deemed the level of affordable housing satisfactory, but this did not outweigh the design harms, while Southwark Council, as the decision maker for the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place, did not consider the affordable housing satisfactory in either development affordable housing was a major bone of contention on all three schemes.  The Aylesham Inspector was satisfied that the 12% affordable housing offer was the maximum reasonable.  Southwark Council's three decisions, though, all cite the low level of affordable housing a reason for refusing the applications [REFS].

### Viability and Berkeley's case

Inevitably the viability of the respective schemes was critical issue for the three applications.  Berkeley's arguments for all the sites were broadly the same - that economic circumstances, including rising build and finance costs, slow sales and new regulatory requirements had rendered their schemes unviable and unable to support the 35% affordable housing required by the Southwark Plan.  Their no-nonsense conclusion (in each case?) was that all three schemes were so unviable that they could not deliver <ins>any</ins> affordable housing (a conclusion the both Southwark's own viability advisors and the GLA agreed with).  Berkeley by *commercial decision* nonetheless proposed to deliver 1O% affordable housing on the Borough Triangle and 12% on the Aylesham Centre site and Bermondsey Place.

The conclusions that the schemes were unviable was challenged by objectors and councillors on several counts.  While the consensus between Berkeley's advisors (DS2), Southwark's advisors (BPS and Quintic) and the GLA (for the Mayor) was that all the schemes were unviable, there was disagreement about the size of the deficits, particularly for those of the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place.  On the Aylesham Centre DS2 found a deficit of £67.35m, while BPS calculated a smaller £41.1m (there is no published figure from the GLA).  On Borough Triangle DS2's deficit was £290.7m, BPS's £79.5m, while the GLA said £131.2m.  On Bermondsey Place DPS said the deficit was £116.84m, Quintic £34.9m, the GLA £3.8m.  As can be seen from these figures the estimates of the deficit varied by £25.95m for the Aylesham Centre and by as much as £211m for Borough Triangle and by as much as £103m for Bermondsey Place.

Objectors and councillors also questioned the higher profit targets proposed by DS2, (20% Gross Development Value (GDV) against the 17.5 % GDV measure used by BPS and the GLA), which raises the viability threshold and diminishes the lillihood of any additional affordable housing, in the event of viability reviews.  They further pointed out that increasing the market housing on the schemes in itself has a detrimental impact on viability, by increasing the length of the development programme (the more market homes there are, the longer it takes to sell them off).  This in turn increases finance costs and whether an 8% finance rate was justified was also questioned, in the case of Borough Triangle.

It was also noted that Berkeley had not taken advantage of the government and Mayor's emergency measures for any of the schemes [LINK], which could have improved viability, by a combination of grant from the Mayor, the proposed relief of Community Infrastructure Levy (CIL) relief, were they willing to commit to 20% affordable housing.  (Berkeley gave a verbal  undertaking at the Bermondsey Place meeting to try to improve the affordable housing beyond 12%, through viability reviews, but this formed no part of the formal offer[REF OR112)

Building on this, Southwark Law Centre suggested that for Bermondsey Place a combination of grant, CIL relief and the 17.5% lower profit level combined could improve viability significantly and possibly be enough to provide 35% affordable housing for Bermondsey Place.  Objectors also pointed out that 35% affordable housing, not just 12%, was being provided on the BeCa development, not many yards down the Old Kent Rd from Bermondsey Place.

### Need versus Viability

An obstacle to establishing whether or not greater viability could be achieved on Bermondsey Place was that Berkeley had failed to test 20% or 35% affordable housing scenarios and their test of 12% showed only that this too was unviable.  Berkeley's commitment to delivering 12% therefore had no underpinning; if the committee had approved the application nonetheless, they would have done so on  nothing more than Berkeley's word that it could be done and without positive evidence that it could include any affordable housing. 

An alternative view is that Berkeley was generously going 'above and beyond' its obligations and their offer should be accepted on the basis that some affordable housing is better than none, which is all there would be if the application is refused.  This argument shows a touching faith in the generousity/benevolence of developers; a more sceptical view might be that 12% is well within Berkeley's powers and the fact that they are offering it, despite their own viability assessment saying it cannot be done profitably, is another reason not to trust viability assessments

This alternative view also ignores several factors.  First, it is in Berkeley's own interests to reduce affordable housing, and so immediately increase profit-bearing market housing.  Secondly, though BPS and the GLA agree the the scheme is unviable, the difference in deficits, with very large ones in two cases, shows that there can be little public confidence in the whole exercise.  There is also a significant lost opportunity cost; 93% households of households in the borough are eligible for affordable housing and 23,000 households are on the housing waiting list.  The 35% affordable housing requirement from private developments has been set to help meet these needs.  But development sites are limited and if they are given over almost entirely to market housing then obviously this will not happen.  And while it is undeniable that, say, 86 households would be housed in affordable homes in the remaining two phases of Bermondsey Place, were it to be built, more will see the prospect of an affordable home recede; not just because the other thousand or so homes will be free-market, but also because the limited amount of land available for housing across the borough will be reduced, without Bermondsey Place providing it's fair share of affordable housing.  The remaining sites,and there are few of Bermondsey Place's size, would have to provide proportionately more affordable homes if the borough's housing needs are to be met.
