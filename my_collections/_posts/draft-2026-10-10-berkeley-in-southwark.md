### Why Berkeley's three housing developments were rejected

23 Oct 2026

<img width="931" height="183" alt="Aylesham_Centre_Borough_Triangle_Bermondsey_Place_composite_121026" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8bc70a4c-93f4-40cb-a622-1cd3096f3954" />

When Southwark Council's planning committee unanimously rejected Berkeley's proposals for the development of Bermondsey Place Phases 2 and 3 at the [beginning of October](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=649&MId=8432&Ver=4), it was the third large Berkeley scheme to fail to gain planning approval, following the Borough Triangle, at the [beginning of September](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=650&MId=8449&Ver=4) and the Aylesham Centre, [in July 2025](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=650&MId=8174&Ver=4).

All three applications entailed big reductions in affordable housing, either by way of a s73 variation of an existing planning permission (reducing affordable housing to 10% on the Borough Triangle), by securing a whole new planning permission (a reduction to 12% Bermondsey Place) or by amending the planning application before it had been determined (also a reduction to 12% for the Aylesham Centre).  Had all three revisions succeeded it would have reduced the number of affordable homes from 951 to 304, across all three schemes, based on available figures, a loss of around **650 affordable homes**, including around **400 social rented homes**[^1].  

<img width="681" height="369" alt="Table_affordable_housing_losses_121026" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/eb26129b-95c3-42a4-8b02-fcbe162e8e15" />

So far, all three rejections still stand.  Berkeley retains the extant (current) permissions for Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place, but would have to deliver 35% affordable housing on each scheme should they build them (increasing to 40% affordable housing on Bermondsey Place, if it gets some grant funding). Berkeley [appealed against the Aylesham Centre decision](https://35percent.org/2026/06/08/aylesham-centre-decision-the-backlash.html)[^2], but lost; an application to the High Court for a judicial review of the Inspector's decision [has also failed](https://www.instagram.com/p/Deti9fMjXab/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&dlrf=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==&srtk=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==).  Berkeley may yet appeal against the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place decisions.

### The three decisions

Affordable housing was a major bone of contention on all three schemes. The Planning Inspector, as decision-maker for the Aylesham, deemed the 12% level of affordable housing satisfactory, but [ruled that this did not outweigh](https://gat04-live-1517c8a4486c41609369c68f30c8-aa81074.divio-media.org/filer_public/03/70/0370039b-2c8d-40c2-be40-4654361c7a8b/appeal_decision_3366760_180526.pdf) the design harms.  On the other hand, Southwark Council, as the decision maker for the [Borough Triangle](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=650&MId=8449&Ver=4) and [Bermondsey Place](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/ieListDocuments.aspx?CId=649&MId=8432&Ver=4), did not consider the affordable housing satisfactory in either development.   

The full reasons for refusing the Borough Triangle application have not been published yet, but the committee report recommended refusal because the 10% affordable housing offer failed to provide enough public benefit to outweigh the heritage harm caused to Trinity Church Square Conservation Area by the two tallest blocks (one of 38 storeys, the other 44 storeys).   

In rejecting the three applications, the planning committee went with the recommendation of their planning officers for the Aylesham Centre and Borough Triangle, but against the recommendation, to approve, in the case of Bermondsey Place Phase 2 and 3.  Also unlike the other two schemes, the construction of Bermondsey Place has already properly begun[^3], with of 285 homes on Phase 1 nearly complete. This first phase will include 81 affordable homes, with all three phases delivering 167 affordable homes, out of 1,453 homes in total.  In percentage terms, Phase 1 will have 40% affordable housing, Phases 2 and 3, 12%, making for 18% affordable housing across the whole site 40%[^4], 

### Viability deficits

Inevitably the viability of the respective schemes was a critical issue for the three applications.  Berkeley's arguments for all the sites were broadly the same - that economic circumstances, including rising build and finance costs, slow sales and new regulatory requirements had rendered their schemes unviable and unable to support the 35% affordable housing required by the Southwark Plan.  Their no-nonsense conclusion was that all three schemes were so unviable that they could not deliver <ins>any</ins> affordable housing (a conclusion the both Southwark's own viability advisors and the GLA agreed with).  Nonetheless, Berkeley took a *commercial decision* on affordable housing in each case - to deliver 10% on the Borough Triangle and 12% affordable housing on the Aylesham Centre and Bermondsey Place.

The conclusions that the schemes were unviable was challenged by objectors and councillors on several counts.  While the consensus between Berkeley's advisors (DS2), Southwark's advisors (BPS and Quintic) and the GLA (for the Mayor) was that all the schemes were unviable, there was disagreement about the size of the deficits, particularly for those of the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place.  

On the Aylesham Centre DS2 found a deficit of £67.35m, while BPS calculated a smaller £41.1m (there is no published figure from the GLA).  On Borough Triangle DS2's deficit was £290.7m, BPS's £79.5m, while the GLA said £131.2m.  On Bermondsey Place DPS said the deficit was £116.84m, Quintic £34.9m, the GLA £3.8m.  As can be seen from these figures the estimates of the deficit varied by £25.95m for the Aylesham Centre and by as much as £211m for Borough Triangle and by as much as £103m for Bermondsey Place[^5].

### Profit and the emergency measure

Objectors and councillors also questioned the higher profit targets proposed by DS2, (20% Gross Development Value (GDV)) against the 17.5 % GDV measure used by BPS and the GLA).  This both raises the viability threshold in the first instance and diminishes the likelihood of any additional affordable housing, in the event of viability reviews. A further concern was that reducing affordable housing, and thereby increasing the market housing, in itself has a detrimental impact on viability.  This is because the greater the  market housing, the longer it takes to sell off, increasing the length of the development programme, which in turn increases finance costs.  The justification for Berkeley's 8% finance rate was also queried, in the cases of the Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place.

Both objectors and councillors also noted that Berkeley had not taken advantage of the [government and Mayor's emergency measures](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/support-for-housebuilding-in-london-package-of-support) for any of the schemes.  The aim of these is to improved viability on *'stalled'* schemes, by a combination of grant from the Mayor and a proposed relief on the Community Infrastructure Levy (CIL), on condition that developers commit to providing 20% affordable housing. Berkeley gave a verbal  undertaking at the Bermondsey Place meeting to try to improve the affordable housing through viability reviews, but this formed no part of the formal offer[^6].

### Affordable housing improves viability

Objectors to Bermondsey Place also noted that Berkeley had not tested higher levels of affordable housing than the offered 12%, such as 20% (the emergency measures level), or 35% (the minimum policy requirement).  While these levels would have reduced the gross value of the scheme, the grant and CIL relief that more affordable housing could attract might compensate for much of this; Southwark Law Centre argued that additional savings from marketing/sales fees, a reduction in finance costs and a lower 17.5% profit rate could leave a scheme with 35% affordable housing in a better viability position than the 12% scheme proposed.

This approach would be in line with the Mayor's current thinking, as laid out in the [draft London Plan 2026](https://www.london.gov.uk/programmes-strategies/planning/london-plan/next-london-plan/draft-london-plan-consultation/draft-london-plan-consultation-resources/draft-london-plan), which says *'affordable housing’s ability to provide cash flow in a development means that providing higher levels of affordable housing forms a vital part of the strategy to increase overall housing delivery. This reduces a site’s exposure to market absorption sales rates, which ultimately limit the number of market homes that can be sold and therefore the overall rate that a site can be built out'*[^7].

The viability test that Berkeley did conduct, of 12% affordable housing (without any of the benefits of grant etc) only served to prove that this was itself <ins>unviable</ins>, which meant that their offer to nonetheless build a scheme with 12% affordable housing had no underpinning.  Had the committee approved the application on this basis, they would have done so relying on Berkeley's word that the scheme was deliverable and without positive evidence that it would include any affordable housing.

Sympathetic supporters[^8] might hold that Berkeley was generously going 'above and beyond' its obligations and their offer should be accepted on the basis that some affordable housing is better than none, which is all there would be if the application is refused.  This argument shows an unwarranted faith in the benevolence of developers; a more sceptical view might be that 12% is well within Berkeley's powers and the fact that they are offering it, despite their own viability assessment saying it cannot be done profitably, is reason to doubt viability assessments

Secondly, though BPS and the GLA agree with D2 that the scheme is unviable, the difference in deficits arrived at by the three bodies different assessments, with very large differences for Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place, can only undermine confidence in the whole exercise.  It certainly demonstrates that the conclusions of viability assessments should be treated with caution.

## Lost opportunities

Had Berkeley's applications been approved Southwark would not only have forsaken nearly 650 affordable homes, but also incurred a significant 'lost opportunity' cost, by allowing three large sites to deliver below the minimum 35% affordable housing requirement.

The affordable housing requirement has been set to help meet the needs of the 93% of households of households in the borough who are eligible for affordable housing[^9] and [22,000 households who are on the housing waiting list](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/news/2026/southwark-prioritise-council-homes-people-most-need).  But development sites are limited and if they are given over almost entirely to market housing then obviously those needs will go unmet. And while it is undeniable that, for example, 86 households would be housed in affordable homes on the remaining two phases of Bermondsey Place, were it to be built, more will see the prospect of an affordable home recede. This is not just because the remaining thousand-plus homes will be free-market, but also because the limited amount of land available for housing in the borough will be reduced, without Bermondsey Place providing its fair share of affordable housing.  The remaining sites, and there are few of Bermondsey Place's size, would have to provide proportionately more affordable homes to make up the loss.

These lost opportunity costs of approving housing developments are also described in the [draft new London Plan 2026](https://www.london.gov.uk/programmes-strategies/planning/london-plan/next-london-plan/draft-london-plan-consultation/draft-london-plan-consultation-resources/draft-london-plan), which says *'Not only do sites delivering low levels of affordable housing mean that the gap between need and supply continues to grow, it poses significant opportunity costs which will continue to impact in the medium to long term given land scarcity in London'*[^10].

### What we say....need must come first

While harm to heritage was the reason for the rejection of the Aylesham Centre scheme, in the eyes of Southwark's planning committee all three of Berkeley's schemes foundered on the lack of affordable housing[^11] and for the simple reason that the schemes do not meet Southwark's housing need.  

Berkeley would have it that any affordable housing should be welcomed, if need is so great, but their argument ignores the suspicion that viability justifications for low affordable housing do not reflect commercial reality, and it ignores the lost opportunity cost of giving over large sites almost entirely to market housing, which few households in Southwark can fully afford.

Berkeley and their supporters also see these decisions as examples of a 'broken planning' system[^12] - but in a borough where a large majority of households would qualify for affordable housing, decisions rejecting schemes that do not provide enough affordable housing are an example of a planning committee that is doing its job, not a symptom of dysfunction.

Finally, Berkeley appears to have believed that simply demonstrating greater unviability in their revised applications would overcome all other considerations.  And, despite affordable housing not being cited amongst the reasons for the greater unviability, Berkeley think it is natural that affordable housing is reduced to solve the problem, while forgetting that the weight to be given to viability is a matter for the decision-maker[^13].  The Aylesham Inspector may have been happy with the affordable housing, but Southwark rightly decided that need comes first on all three sites, and they must defend their Borough Triangle and Bermondsey Place decisions robustly, if they are challenged by Berkeley.
